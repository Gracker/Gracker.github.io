This article introduces Choreographer, a class that app developers rarely encounter but is crucial in the Android Framework rendering pipeline. It covers the background of Choreographer‘s introduction, an overview, source code analysis, its relationship with MessageQueue, its use in APM, and optimization ideas from mobile manufacturers.

Choreographer coordinates animations, some input processing, and View updates with display timing. It requests frame callbacks when work is pending rather than unconditionally waking the app on every physical refresh. A 60Hz display, common in 2019, has an interval of about 16.67ms; 90Hz and 120Hz correspond to about 11.11ms and 8.33ms. Modern devices can also change refresh rates. A timely callback does not mean the image has been presented: RenderThread, GPU, composition, and display work remain. Understanding this flow helps explain Message, Handler, Looper, MessageQueue, Measure, Layout, and Draw.

Version note — reviewed 2026-10-13: Historical excerpts use AOSP android-10.0.0_r1 Choreographer as a reference and retain the 2019 screenshots. Updates cover Android 16 and API 24/31/33 observation interfaces. Source excerpts are not standalone compilable apps; vendor implementations and capture configurations are incomplete. Obsolete private interfaces are marked below.

This is the eighth article in the Systrace series, providing a brief introduction to Choreographer within Systrace.

The purpose of this series is to view the overall operation of the Android system from a different perspective using Systrace, while also providing an alternative angle for learning the Framework. Perhaps you’ve read many articles about the Framework but can never remember the code, or you’re unclear about the execution flow. Maybe from Systrace’s graphical perspective, you can gain a deeper understanding.

Essence of the Main Thread Mechanism

This article connects to Perfetto 5: Choreographer-based rendering flow. Looper dispatches Messages; MessageQueue does not itself execute app code. Native polling can also process file-descriptor events while obtaining the next message, including display events. “Every main-thread operation is a Message” is therefore an oversimplification.

In Perfetto, correlate the frame with the main thread, RenderThread, GPU, and SurfaceFlinger. On Android 12 / API 31 or later, with the relevant data captured, FrameTimeline expected/actual tracks help locate missed deadlines. Missing tracks do not mean zero jank. Investigate Sleeping, Runnable, and Running separately; a slice’s wall duration is not CPU time. These historical images show spaced dispatches, and method instrumentation itself affects timing.

Evolution

Choreographer arrived in Android 4.1 / API 16 to coordinate work with display timing. Both images below already contain Choreographer.doFrame and RenderThread. They cannot prove behavior before Choreographer existed. They show a different capture with denser rendering and waits; its precise configuration is unavailable.

MethodTrace Illustration

The crop includes RenderThread dequeueBuffer intervals, but does not establish the sole bottleneck or a fixed consumption rate. Buffer availability, consumer progress, and fences require investigation; SurfaceFlinger does not simply consume a “Front Buffer” at an invariant rate.

Consistent presentation matters, but stable 50fps is not universally smoother than fluctuating 40–60fps. On a fixed 60Hz display, 50fps itself cannot be presented uniformly in whole refresh intervals. VSync coordination and buffering improve pacing opportunities; they do not guarantee a stable rate or remove the workload.

Introducing Choreographer

Choreographer requests the next applicable VSync event and schedules callbacks on its Looper thread. It does not set the panel’s refresh rate, and app VSync callbacks are not hardware interrupt handlers. A normal View window with no pending work need not continuously produce new frames.

Read this as a simplified historical diagram. RenderThread is a CPU thread, so labeling its entire box “GPU” is inaccurate. Buffer acquisition, GPU submission, queuing, and composition are not always serialized as drawn. The 16.6ms interval approximates 60Hz, not every stage’s complete budget or end-to-end input latency. See the 90Hz discussion.

Choreographer Overview

Choreographer executes due frame callbacks in stages and requests display events when needed. Android 10 orders them INPUT → ANIMATION → INSETS_ANIMATION → TRAVERSAL → COMMIT. It does not collect every app Message, nor does every frame necessarily perform full measure/layout/draw.

Choreographer, SurfaceFlinger, and buffering participate in scheduling; they cannot guarantee 20, 60, or 90fps. Monitoring must distinguish public FrameCallback, hidden FrameInfo, Looper dispatch timing, and presentation data. IdleHandler runs under queue-idle conditions, not reliably at the end of every frame.

Diagrams are useful, but I don’t particularly enjoy drawing them. I spend more time looking at Systrace and MethodTrace in my day-to-day work: CPU, SurfaceFlinger, SystemServer, and App activity laid out from left to right. Once you know the code, it becomes easier to connect the trace with what is happening on the phone. So I’ll mainly use Systrace here.

Choreographer Workflow from a Systrace Perspective

This Launcher scroll is a historical example. A green F is an old Systrace frame-analysis marker, not independent proof of successful on-screen presentation. The alternating background represents that trace’s VSync reference; check the axis and configuration instead of assuming 16.6ms from its colors.

On a typical hardware-accelerated View path, the main thread updates drawing state and RenderThread prepares/submits rendering work. Buffer queuing must still be correlated with GPU completion, SurfaceFlinger composition, and presentation. UI and RenderThread work can overlap; adding their wall durations does not give display latency.

Zooming into one historical frame shows Choreographer’s callbacks and the handoff to RenderThread. The old annotations are qualified below:

Choreographer Workflow

  1. Create the thread’s Choreographer, bind Looper/FrameHandler, and initialize its display receiver and callback queues.
  2. When due work needs a frame and none is scheduled, request VSync. onVsync posts an asynchronous Runnable to that Looper; its later run() invokes doFrame().
  3. doFrame checks whether work remains scheduled, measures its start delay relative to frame time, and adjusts animation time when needed.
  4. Execute due INPUT, ANIMATION, INSETS_ANIMATION, TRAVERSAL, and COMMIT callbacks in order. Traversal may synchronize drawing state with RenderThread.
  5. RenderThread submits GPU work and a Buffer. Submission returning does not imply GPU completion; fences communicate completion/dependencies for subsequent composition and presentation.
  6. Schedule another frame only if work such as animation continues.

The old annotation above calls Traversal the third callback and omits Insets Animation; it is the fourth category in Android 10. Its “rendering complete, send Buffer” caption also needs the asynchronous GPU/fence qualification above.

FrameDisplayEventReceiver Overview

FrameDisplayEventReceiver handles Vsync registration, requests, and reception. It inherits from DisplayEventReceiver and has three key methods:

  1. onVsync – Vsync signal callback
  2. run – Executes doFrame
  3. scheduleVsync – Requests the Vsync signal

scheduleVsync() is implemented by the parent DisplayEventReceiver; code accessing its private mReceiverPtr does not belong inside the subclass. FrameHandler’s MSG_DO_FRAME is principally the timer fallback when VSync is disabled; the normal VSync path uses the Runnable above.

Logic for Processing One Frame

The core logic resides in Choreographer.doFrame. FrameDisplayEventReceiver.onVsync posts itself, and its run method calls doFrame:

android/view/Choreographer.java

doFrame performs three main tasks:

  1. Estimates dispatch-start delay relative to frame time.
  2. Records frame drawing information.
  3. Executes CALLBACK_INPUT, CALLBACK_ANIMATION, CALLBACK_INSETS_ANIMATION, CALLBACK_TRAVERSAL, and CALLBACK_COMMIT.

Dropped Frame Logic

The calculation is startNanos - frameTimeNanos: how late this doFrame starts relative to the supplied frame timestamp. Integer division estimates missed scheduling intervals, not this frame’s rendering duration or the full count of missed presentations. The surrounding source also adjusts animation frame time and handles backward time.

The diagram’s start_time should mean the timestamp carried by the event, not when Java happens to receive it. Its divisor must not be hardcoded to 16ms.

Earlier work can delay the next callback, but this is not simply a detector for “the previous frame.” GPU, composition, or presentation misses may produce no such warning. Runnable scheduling delays can contribute without the app continuously running on CPU. The final annotation also reverses the subtraction; the source subtracts the frame timestamp from dispatch-start time.

Source Code Summary

  1. getInstance() returns the calling Looper thread’s instance, not a process-wide singleton; a missing Looper causes an exception.
  2. Requests go through the display connection, events through FD/Looper dispatch, and doFrame through an asynchronous Message. None is an app hardware-interrupt handler.
  3. FrameCallback is one-shot and runs in ANIMATION. One callback does not guarantee one new Buffer.
  4. Android 10/16 order due work as INPUT, ANIMATION, INSETS_ANIMATION, TRAVERSAL, COMMIT. Earlier updates can affect the later traversal.
  5. COMMIT runs after traversal, not after GPU/SF completion or presentation. ActivityThread.scheduleTrimMemory() uses it when the main Choreographer exists, but this is one use, not an onTrimMemory call every frame.
  6. ListView obtain/setup can be driven by input scrolling, continuing animation, or layout. Read the actual stack rather than inferring the cause from a stage name.

APM and Choreographer

These interfaces observe different stages; their numbers are not interchangeable FPS or dropped-frame counts:

  • FrameCallback observes callback pacing on its Looper, not Buffer/presentation counts.
  • gfxinfo ... framestats exposes window HWUI statistics with version-specific fields.
  • SurfaceFlinger --latency is a historical diagnostic whose target layer and implementation must match.
  • The private PageFlip query is historical and unavailable under its old code in Android 16.
  • Choreographer skip warnings indicate dispatch-start lateness, not complete presentation behavior.
  • Looper/BlockCanary measures message dispatch, not displayed frames.

Android 7 / API 24 provides Window.OnFrameMetricsAvailableListener. Its dropCountSinceLastInvocation counts dropped metric notifications, not dropped display frames. On Android 12 / API 31 and later, where captured, Perfetto FrameTimeline links expected/actual app and SF frames to help investigate CPU, GPU, and composition delays.

Using FrameCallback.doFrame

Use it on a thread with a Looper, normally the main thread for View apps:

postFrameCallback() is public from API 16 and one-shot. A continuous observer must repost and stop appropriately; the observer itself requests more frames, so callback counts are not necessarily app draw counts. frameTimeNanos is the animation frame time, possibly jitter-adjusted, not the instant execution starts.

TinyDancer was the historical callback-based pacing project referenced here. Its displayed rate is not automatically composited screen FPS. API 33 adds postVsyncCallback() with FrameData/FrameTimeline information such as candidate deadlines and expected presentation times. These are predictions, not measured presentation; use the data within the callback’s valid lifetime.

Using FrameInfo

Substitute the actual package. The preserved sample below says Window: StatusBar; it must not be presented as verified output of that Launcher command in the same run.

Keep and parse the CSV header: Android/HWUI versions change fields. Timestamps use the implementation’s monotonic clock in nanoseconds; Duration columns are durations, not timestamps. The old OldestInputEvent=9223372036854775807 and NewestInputEvent=0 combination indicates no valid input timestamps, not enormous input latency. FrameCompleted is not screen presentation. Slow-frame categories can overlap and must not be added as disjoint bad-frame counts.

Using SurfaceFlinger

These are Android 10-era diagnostic examples. List actual layers and query the relevant one; names change with windows, recreation, and versions:

In Android 10 SurfaceFlinger::dumpStatsLocked() and FrameTracker::dumpStats(), the first line is the refresh interval, followed by columns:

  1. A: desiredPresentTime, desired presentation time.
  2. B: actualPresentTime, recorded actual presentation time.
  3. C: frameReadyTime, when contents became ready for presentation, possibly obtained from a ready fence.

These are not app draw start, pre-submission VSync, and hardware acceptance. That implementation uses a 128-entry ring but dumps 127 slots per tracker. Zero and pending INT64_MAX entries are not valid frames. Check the target, time range, validity, monotonicity, gaps, and refresh changes rather than assuming 128 new frames per command.

The old ceil((C - A) / refresh_period) formula and “a change means jank” rule are not universal. Desired, ready, and actual times measure different things; even persistently high latency can leave that value unchanged. Analyze presentation intervals and expected deadlines according to the implementation, preferably correlating FrameTimeline where supported.

Using SurfaceFlinger PageFlip

Historical private interface, not a current app-monitoring recipe. Android 10’s private debug transaction 1013 returned the default display’s PageFlip count. Even with access, that was display-level activity, not one app’s frame count. The old command also omitted shell; correcting it to adb shell service call SurfaceFlinger 1013 does not make it public or remove permission restrictions.

Android 16 source explicitly contains:

The old example that read transact(1013) as a count and calculated app FPS is therefore removed. Use public window metrics or version-appropriate trace data. Never ignore transaction/return status or treat unsupported output as zero frames.

Choreographer Skip Warnings

Android 10 defaults SKIPPED_FRAME_WARNING_LIMIT to 30 via debug.choreographer.skipwarning, read when the class initializes. This is a logging threshold, not proof that smaller delays are smooth or that every frame has been measured.

Frame time, dispatch start, work completion, and presentation remain separate. Changing the warning threshold does not fix latency.

BlockCanary

Tools such as BlockCanary time the boundaries of Looper message dispatch. This is an excerpt of the older dispatch path:

It measures dispatch wall time including waits, not pure CPU time or the complete RenderThread/GPU/SF chain. It also does not cover every native-poll callback outside dispatch. A backlog of individually short messages can delay work without one long-message warning. Logging, string construction, and sampling have overhead, and setMessageLogging must coexist with other observers. This is not zero-overhead, complete jank detection.

MessageQueue and Choreographer

In Android 10 MessageQueue.next(), a queue-head barrier with target == null makes the queue search forward for an asynchronous message, still respecting its when. Synchronous messages behind the barrier wait for removal. This neither preempts a running message nor raises CPU scheduling priority.

Message.setAsynchronous() is public from API 22, and Handler.createAsync() from API 28. “Asynchronous” means exempt from synchronization barriers, not automatically first, on another thread, or immediate. Without a barrier, normal queue timing/order still applies. The framework’s barrier insertion/removal APIs are not public app SDK contracts.

Manufacturer Optimizations

Working on the system side gives us room to change source code and try scheduling policies. I can’t share the proprietary implementation here, but I can outline the ideas. If you’re interested, we can discuss them privately. The examples below do not include controlled results.

Input-event Optimization

AOSP already has CALLBACK_INPUT and batched-input consumption. The historical idea concerns changing input scheduling or responding earlier, not adding input support to a Choreographer that has none. Earlier work must be evaluated alongside batching, resampling, power, and presentation pacing. It is not automatically better.

Background Animation Optimization

An app’s self-reposting callbacks or animations can continue creating work when no longer useful. Decisions to stop them should account for lifecycle, window visibility, multi-window operation, and the actual feature; not every background callback is meaningless.

I recorded this historical com.netease.newsreader.activity capture as a background case. It shows dense frame activity but no independent visibility or power evidence, and makes no claim about the current app. Apps should cancel unneeded animations/callbacks; platform policies must also preserve legitimate visible-window and background behavior.

Frame Scheduling Optimization

“Compose without waiting for VSync” was an OEM exploration direction, not a public app switch. Scheduling still depends on buffers, fences, display hardware, and refresh constraints. Immediate scheduling can increase power or disrupt pacing without reducing end-to-end latency. No implementation or comparison is provided, so this is not a verified optimization result.

App Startup Optimization

First locate long work, locks, and queues on the launch critical path. System-level message reordering must preserve lifecycle and dependencies; moving arbitrary “important” messages to the front risks incorrect order and starvation. Apps should reduce critical-path work, defer unnecessary initialization, and measure first-frame and interactive readiness separately.

High-refresh-rate Optimization

60/90/120Hz correspond to about 16.67/11.11/8.33ms; the original 8.8ms for 120Hz was incorrect. Refresh intervals are not a full independent budget for every thread. Evaluate app/game load, rate transitions, thermals, power, and pacing together rather than requiring every app to render at maximum rate continuously.
