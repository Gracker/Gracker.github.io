What are Sleep States in Linux?

TASK_INTERRUPTIBLE vs. TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE

A thread which is neither Running nor Runnable is not necessarily sleeping: it may be stopped, traced, or exiting. The following are three common waiting semantics, not an exhaustive list of Linux states:

  • TASK_INTERRUPTIBLE: the wait can be woken by its condition or an applicable pending signal.
  • TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE: ordinary signals or SIGKILL alone do not end the wait; a signal may remain pending until the wait returns.
  • TASK_KILLABLE: combines TASK_WAKEKILL | TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE and responds to signals the kernel classifies as fatal, notably SIGKILL. TASK_WAKEKILL is a flag, not a separate sleeping mechanism.

Figure 1 is a simplified model. Historical viewers commonly label interruptible waits Sleep and distinguish Uninterruptible Sleep, but colors vary by viewer and theme. A sleeping thread is not executing on a CPU; after wakeup it must be scheduled again.

“Uninterruptible” does not mean permanently unwakeable or hardware interrupts disabled. Completion, an explicit wakeup, or a timeout supported by the particular wait API can resume the task. For example, wait_event_timeout uses an uninterruptible wait with a timeout. See the Linux 6.12 wait APIs and Linux 5.10 state definitions.

Looper polling and userspace futex waits commonly appear as Sleep, but Java/native locks and network operations do not all imply one kernel state. Inspect the actual path and wait API. An idle main thread waiting for messages is normally healthy; replacing that wait with busy polling is not an optimization.

The Purpose of TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE

Interruptibility here concerns how a wait responds to signals. POSIX signals, softirqs, and hardware IRQs are different mechanisms with different execution contexts; a sleeping thread is not simultaneously executing hardware interrupt handlers.

A kernel operation may use an uninterruptible wait because it has no ordinary-signal cancellation semantics or must await an internal condition. This is distinct from disabling preemption or local CPU interrupts. TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE does not itself disable an interrupt controller.

I/O completion, some kernel locks, page waits, and driver synchronization can use such waits. Not every I/O operation, network access, or lock wait enters D. Inspect the target kernel’s actual wait function; Linux 5.10 is a versioned reference, while a changing “latest” page cannot establish the implementation behind an old screenshot.

Analysis Philosophy

TASK_INTERRUPTIBLE and TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE are normal. A high sleeping percentage can simply mean idle work. Investigate when waiting delays a required result or deadline. Master these two points:

  1. Troubleshooting Methodology
  2. Optimization Methodology

You must identify if a sleep is active or passive. If active, is the logic sound? If passive, what is the source? This requires deep system knowledge and experience.

Note: I’ll use “Sleep” for TASK_INTERRUPTIBLE and “UninterruptibleSleep” for TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE to match Systrace terminology.

It is easy to get lost when you first analyze Sleep and UninterruptibleSleep. Reading Framework and kernel code helps you make sense of the clues in a trace. The trace only covers the events that were captured, and Systrace’s wakeup_from does not by itself identify a lock owner or the complete business dependency. You need both an understanding of the system and the trace to explain the cause. Otherwise, you end up doing what I call ‘performance optimization by bluffing’: you assert a cause that nobody can check, send the testers through another round of retesting, and let the issue disappear when they cannot reproduce it.

This article covers common types and cases. Use these diagnostic methods and source code analysis to navigate unique problems.

Diagnostic Methods

Use wakeup from tid: *** to Find Waking Threads

As discussed in the Runnable article, waking up and actually executing are separate steps. Timers, device completion, and interrupt paths can wake tasks as well as other threads. Preemption, yielding, and newly enqueued tasks also contribute runnable work; the original two-case classification was incomplete.

wakeup from tid identifies the wakeup context recorded by scheduler events. It is not necessarily the business-level producer or the owner of a contested lock. Interrupt context, proxy wakeups, multiple waiters, and missing events can make a simple tid attribution misleading.

The red annotations are historical. In particular, “Running instead” does not prove the other task had higher priority or caused the delay; it helps identify other work running at that time. The shown wakeup from tid: 14116 is a lead to investigate.

Current Perfetto can correlate sched_switch, sched_waking, and thread-state details. Depending on the capture, tables may expose waker_utid and irq_context. Missing values do not establish that there was no waker. See scheduler events and the thread_state table.

Other Methods

  1. Stacks and off-CPU analysis: ordinary CPU sampling mainly observes stacks while the thread runs; it does not reconstruct a complete execution flow during sleep. With supported kernels, permissions, and versions, simpleperf’s --trace-offcpu combines scheduling information with stacks at descheduling. It still does not record every function entry and exit. See the official simpleperf reference.
  2. Event correlation: use timing to identify candidates, then confirm with Binder transactions, futex addresses, application identifiers, kernel stacks, or source. Simultaneous events alone do not establish causality.

Ask what condition is awaited, who advances it, when it becomes satisfied, and how long scheduling takes afterward, rather than merely matching colors on timelines.

Common Causes for Long Sleep

  • Binder: a synchronous call may wait for remote queueing, execution, and a reply. Follow transaction links and distinguish service-side execution, lock waits, and CPU waits. A oneway call does not wait for a synchronous reply in the same way.
  • Java/futex locks and conditions: contention may occur in apps or services, but a futex is a mechanism and does not always mean a mutex wait. Identify the actual condition, lock, and dependency.
  • Intentional waits: timers, semaphores, and message queues can provide necessary pacing. Establish whether the wait delays a required result before trying to remove it.
  • Graphics fences: a fence can track GPU, display, or buffer use; waiting does not prove the GPU is continuously busy. Identify the producer, submission, and signal times, then consider shader, resolution, or texture reductions where relevant. Increasing GPU frequency is not a general app-level fix. See the AOSP synchronization framework.

Analyzing the Uninterruptible Sleep State

Diagnostic Methods

First establish a D-class wait, then look for I/O classification and a blocked function. These are optional capture details, not properties that every UninterruptibleSleep interval necessarily exposes:

  1. The I/O-wait flag can be 0, 1, or absent.
  2. Block reason depends on kernel tracepoints, capture configuration, symbols, and parser support.

IOWait vs. Non-IOWait

Here I/O wait is a kernel flag recorded for the task, not a complete test for any I/O performed by an operation, nor the CPU-usage statistic called iowait. iowait=0 means this point was not marked as I/O waiting; it does not rule out I/O elsewhere in the operation. A missing field is not zero.

A page-cache miss may cause storage access, while a cache hit may still require page-lock, writeback, or other synchronization. One syscall may include several running and waiting intervals. Non-IO candidates include kernel locks, page waits, and driver conditions; Non-IO is not synonymous with a kernel-lock wait.

Common Causes for IOWait and Optimizations

1. Active IO Operations

Candidates include synchronous reads/writes, fsync, filesystem metadata, competing storage users, and the actual swap backend. Distinguish them using request sizes, access patterns, latency, queues, and errors. A D interval alone does not diagnose fragmentation, flash aging, or low free space.

Optimization methods:

  • Reduce critical-path synchronous I/O, combine unnecessary small requests, and batch appropriately. After making work asynchronous, check that the wait was not merely moved onto another critical dependency.
  • Readahead helps predictable locality; excessive prefetch consumes bandwidth and memory. Measure the target workload instead of always increasing it.
  • Page-cache reclaim, system-file residency, and storage-maintenance policies belong to platform configuration. PinFile or locking many pages is not a general app solution and can crowd out other work.
  • An I/O benchmark describes its own conditions, not the complete application workload, cache state, temperature, and filesystem behavior.

Swap-in need not read physical storage. Android commonly uses compressed RAM through zRAM, with CPU decompression cost. zRAM configured with a backing device/writeback may also use storage. Check the actual configuration; see Android memory management and zRAM.

2. High IO due to Low Memory

File-page caching reduces repeated reads. Memory pressure may cause more eviction, faults, reclaim, or writeback, but it does not mean almost every I/O bypasses memory and hits disk. Distinguish file-backed and anonymous pages, clean and dirty pages, and the swap backend.

Optimization methods:

  • Reduce synchronous I/O on critical paths and organize data/prefetch for the measured access pattern.
  • Avoid unbounded business caches, unnecessary residency, and excessive short-lived allocations.
  • At the platform level, correlate reclaim, faults, storage, and memory-pressure events. Distinguish useful cache occupancy from pressure delaying work rather than maximizing free RAM.

System memory is shared by all processes. An app that grabs resources without considering its neighbors can hurt them, then suffer itself when the system intervenes. Expecting every developer to account for all of this is unrealistic. I think clear, enforced platform rules may be the way out.

Common Causes for Non-IOWait

  • Reclaim or page waits: pressure can involve locks, page waits, and writeback, with either Non-IO or I/O-marked intervals. Inspect the actual stack and events.
  • Binder kernel paths: particular operations or locks can produce this state, but waiting for a synchronous Binder reply commonly appears as ordinary Sleep. Transaction volume alone is not a diagnosis.
  • Other kernel locks, drivers, and completion conditions: Non-IO is a starting classification, not the final cause.

System Scheduling and UninterruptibleSleep Coupling

If a verified lock owner spends a long time Runnable, its scheduling delay can extend another thread’s lock wait; priority inversion is one possible case. Establish ownership first instead of inferring it from Non-IO or wakeup from.

Shorten critical sections, avoid expensive work while holding locks, and investigate why the dependency is not running. Linux provides RT-mutex / PI-futex priority inheritance for applicable locks, but not every mutex, rwsem, or futex wait automatically has PI. It does not replace sound locking design, and scheduler modification is not the only possible remedy.

Appendix

Linux Thread State Definitions

These are common Trace display symbols, not an exact mapping of every kernel version’s /proc state characters. Parsers also support historical combinations; use the actual event and viewer version.

Display symbol Meaning and limits
S Sleeping, typically an interruptible wait
R Runnable; raw Linux TASK_RUNNING also includes execution, which the viewer separates as Running
R+ Runnable after preemption
D Uninterruptible Sleep; alone it does not establish I/O or fatal-signal behavior
T / t Stopped / traced
Z Zombie
X / x Historical exit/task-dead representations
K Wake-kill flag, potentially combined with other states; not a synonym for D
W Waking, depending on kernel and parser reporting
I / P / N Idle / Parked / No-load markers, version-dependent
Unknown or absent fields Insufficient data, not automatically Sleep, Non-IO, or zero wait
